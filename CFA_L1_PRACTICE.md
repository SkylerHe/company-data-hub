**Q1 — 6.1 Market Organization & Structure** 🖥️
An order to **buy** that becomes valid only once the price *rises* to a specified level, and then executes at the best available price, is *best* described as a:
- A. Buy limit order
- B. Buy stop order
- C. Market-on-open order

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B is correct.** A buy stop is dormant until price rises to the stop, then converts to a market order — used to enter on upside breakouts or cover a short.
- A: a buy *limit* sits *below* the market and caps the price you'll pay; it doesn't wait for price to rise.
- C: a market-on-open executes in the opening auction, with no trigger price.

</details>

**Q2 — 6.1 Market Organization & Structure (margin)** 🖥️
An investor buys 100 shares at **$50** using **50% initial margin**. The price rises to **$60**. Ignoring interest and commissions, the return on the investor's *equity* is *closest to*:
- A. 20%
- B. 33%
- C. 40%

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C is correct.** Position = 100 × $50 = $5,000; equity = 50% = $2,500, loan = $2,500. At $60 the position is $6,000; equity = $6,000 − $2,500 = $3,500. Return = ($3,500 − $2,500) / $2,500 = **40%**.
- A (20%) is just the stock's price move — it ignores leverage.
- B (33%) is a miscalculation. Leverage of 2× turns a +20% move into ~+40% (and magnifies losses the same way).
</details>

**Q3 — 6.1 Market Organization & Structure**
In a **quote-driven** (dealer) market, investors most likely trade with:
- A. Each other, via a central limit order book
- B. Dealers, who post bid and ask prices
- C. Brokers, who act only as agents

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B is correct.** In quote-driven markets, dealers supply liquidity by quoting bids/asks and trade as principals.
- A describes an *order-driven* market (investor orders matched in a book).
- C: brokers are agents, but the defining counterparty in a quote-driven market is the dealer.
</details>

**Q4 — 6.2 Security Market Indexes**
In a **price-weighted** index, the constituent with the greatest influence on the index is the one with the:
- A. Highest share price
- B. Largest market capitalization
- C. Greatest number of shares outstanding

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A is correct.** A price-weighted index (e.g., the DJIA) weights by *price*, so the highest-priced share moves it most, regardless of company size.
- B describes a *market-cap-weighted* index (e.g., the S&P 500 / SPY).
- C is irrelevant to price weighting.
</details>

**Q5 — 6.2 Security Market Indexes**
Periodically changing **which securities are included** in an index is *best* described as:
- A. Rebalancing
- B. Reconstitution
- C. Reweighting

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B is correct.** Reconstitution changes membership (adds/removes constituents).
- A (rebalancing) resets *existing* constituents back to target weights.
- C is not the standard term for either.
</details>

**Q6 — 6.3 Market Efficiency**
In a market where **technical analysis** cannot earn abnormal returns but **fundamental analysis** can, the market is *best* described as:
- A. Weak-form efficient
- B. Semi-strong-form efficient
- C. Strong-form efficient

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A is correct.** Weak-form efficiency means prices reflect all *past price/volume* data, so technical analysis fails — but fundamental analysis on public data can still add value.
- B would also negate fundamental analysis on *public* information.
- C would negate even private (insider) information.
</details>

**Q7 — 6.3 Market Efficiency (behavioral)**
An investor who holds losing positions too long and sells winning positions too early is exhibiting:
- A. The disposition effect
- B. Anchoring
- C. Confirmation bias

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A is correct.** The disposition effect is the reluctance to realize losses and eagerness to realize gains.
- B (anchoring): over-relying on an initial reference value.
- C (confirmation bias): seeking information that supports a prior belief.

**Q8 — 6.4 Overview of Equity Securities**
Compared with common shares, **preferred shares** most likely:
- A. Offer higher expected capital appreciation
- B. Carry greater voting rights
- C. Have a claim to dividends senior to that of common shares

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C is correct.** Preferred shareholders receive their (typically fixed) dividends before common and rank ahead in liquidation.
- A: common shares have the greater upside/appreciation potential.
- B: preferred shares usually carry *limited or no* voting rights.
</details>

**Q9 — 6.4 Overview of Equity Securities**
A **putable** common share is most beneficial to the:
- A. Issuing company
- B. Shareholder
- C. Underwriter

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B is correct.** A putable share lets the *shareholder* sell it back to the issuer at a pre-set price, protecting downside.
- A describes a *callable* share, which benefits the issuer.
- C is irrelevant; underwriters aren't party to the put feature.
</details>

**Q10 — 6.5 Company Analysis: Past and Present**
A company that consistently earns a **return on invested capital (ROIC) above its weighted average cost of capital (WACC)** most likely has:
- A. A sustainable competitive advantage
- B. Excessive financial leverage
- C. A low dividend payout ratio

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A is correct.** Earning ROIC > WACC over time means the firm creates value beyond its cost of capital — the hallmark of a durable competitive advantage (an economic moat).
- B: leverage affects ROE and risk, not whether returns exceed the cost of capital.
- C: payout policy is a distribution decision, unrelated to value creation.
</details>

**Q11 — 6.6 Industry and Competitive Analysis**
Under Porter's five forces, the **threat of new entrants** into an industry is *lowest* when:
- A. Barriers to entry are high
- B. Customer switching costs are low
- C. Industry growth is rapid

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A is correct.** High barriers to entry (scale, capital, patents, brand) deter newcomers, lowering the threat.
- B: low switching costs make it *easier* to lure customers, raising the threat.
- C: rapid growth attracts entrants, raising the threat.
</details>

**Q12 — 6.7 Company Analysis: Forecasting**
An analyst who first forecasts total **industry sales** and then estimates the company's **market share** to project its revenue is using a:
- A. Top-down approach
- B. Bottom-up approach
- C. Hybrid approach

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A is correct.** Top-down forecasting starts with the macro/industry picture and narrows to the company (industry sales × market share).
- B (bottom-up) builds from company-specific drivers (units, price, capacity) upward.
- C (hybrid) blends both; here only the top-down chain is described.
</details>

**Q13 — 6.8 Equity Valuation: Concepts and Basic Tools**
A stock is expected to pay a **$1.50** dividend next year, grow dividends at **4%** forever, and has a required return of **9%**. Using the Gordon growth model, its intrinsic value is *closest to*:
- A. $17
- B. $30
- C. $38

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B is correct.** V₀ = D₁ / (r − g) = $1.50 / (0.09 − 0.04) = $1.50 / 0.05 = **$30**.
- A (~$17) wrongly adds g to r in the denominator (0.09 + 0.04 → 0.13).
- C (~$38) uses too small a denominator. The model needs r > g to be valid.

</details>
